Parks don't just happen. They are a team effort, with great dividends. A community working together on a park
project builds more than a park — they're building stronger community bonds, creating engaging employment
opportunities for local youth, and making everyone proud. Park building brings people together around a common
cause. And when it's done, they will say, "we did it!"

At the core of every successful park project is a diverse and talented group of committed residents working
together as a team. This Park Committee will lead your park creation effort. Ideally, these volunteers represent
the various groups who will use the park — young, old, different racial and ethnic groups, professional, working
class, homeowners and renters. They should look like your community. Additionally, think about their talents.
Having people with different talents and experience — builders, legal knowledge, political connections, community
organizers, clergy, social butterflies, etc. — will increase your chances of success.

## Neighborhood assets {/* #neighborhood-assets */}

Another critical step in your park-building journey is identifying the unique features of your neighborhood and
park site. Take time to stop and look for the abundance of clues that make your neighborhood special in its own
way. Every neighborhood has inherent value born of its own history and culture. Identifying and tapping into these
assets will strengthen your park project and inspire community ownership.

So how do we identify assets and strengths? Think about your neighborhood and its history and culture — what are
the special places? Where do people like to congregate? Were there important events that happened in your
neighborhood? What once stood where your park is located?

## Organize a meeting {/* #organize-a-meeting */}

Holding a public meeting can be a really good way of building a campaign or getting more people involved in your
group. Here are a few ideas to help your community group organize a meeting that is interesting, accessible and
runs smoothly.

- **Keep it simple.** Don't try to cram in too much. Use the meeting to share basic information about what you
  want to do, encourage others to share their opinions and ideas, and gain contact details of people who may wish
  to be involved in the future.
- **Publicize it well.** Make sure the date, time and place are clearly shown on the flyer, and that it's clear
  what the meeting is about. Flyers, posters, social media posts, and your local community newsletter all help.
- **Offer an incentive.** Free refreshments, a video or speaker, or childcare with student volunteers can help
  people make the time to come.
- **Think about the agenda.** Welcome and introductions; explain who called the meeting and why; outline why the
  meeting has been called; consider asking someone who has built a park to share their insights (keep it brief —
  this first meeting shouldn't be a lecture); ask the Park in a Truck team at Jefferson to provide a speaker.
- **Give people a chance to share.** There may be opposition to a park — let people air their views and build
  consensus on the possible park location, without getting bogged down in the details.
- **Say what happens next.** Summarize the ideas raised, and if the meeting is small, reach decisions about what
  action is actually going to be taken — including who is going to do it.
- **Chair the meeting.** Agree beforehand who will chair. Their job: keep the meeting on topic, make sure people get
  a chance to have their say, and keep the atmosphere welcoming and open.
- **Take minutes.** Decide who will take minutes before the meeting (not the same person as the chair). Minutes
  should include a clear note of any decisions made, and who has agreed to take on which jobs.
- **Gather names and addresses.** Prepare a sign-up sheet in advance so you can keep in touch with everyone who
  wants to stay involved.
- **Plan your next meeting.** Fix a date and venue for your group's next meeting before this one ends, and
  distribute it so people can come back.
