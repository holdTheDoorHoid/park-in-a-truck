What makes your lot special, and how can you best use these features? This step reviews how to evaluate your lot
and ensure your park takes advantage of its site conditions.

A lot doesn't stand alone — it's part of your community. So assessment needs to include your neighborhood too. Is
there a unique history or culture that should be reflected in the design? A park that pays homage to a community's
sense of itself will feel more like an old neighbor rather than a newcomer.

Example project sites are used to illustrate the key steps in this process. Of course, your situation will be
different, depending on the location, shape, and context of your own park site — but this is a great place to
start.

## Site characteristics

In this step, you will document the physical characteristics of the park site. Is the site sunny or shady? Flat or
sloped, with existing trees? Or is it across the street from a church? All of these characteristics will influence
what you can do on the site. For example, suppose you want to grow tomatoes — your site will need to be sunny
throughout the day.

Visit your park site and carefully document what might affect your park's design. By evaluating the site
characteristics before designing the park's features, you'll make sure the design is adapted to the site's unique
conditions.

This example lot is unique because of: **(1)** its location on a busy corner, **(2)** being on a bus route, **(3)** a
building with a large blank wall at the end, **(4)** utility lines along the street edge, and **(5)** a sunny area
along the sidewalk. Your lot will have its own list. Document any cultural or historical features of the space and
adjacent blocks, too — did an interesting business once occupy the lot? Were there historical events nearby? What
did the space look like before there were buildings? This is a great way to engage long-time residents and students
who have the research skills to dive into city archives.

## Site visit

The following activities can be done as a group or individually when you visit the park site:

1. **Collect soil samples.** Understanding the soil nutrients is very helpful in determining what may grow best in
   your park. A simple test for lead will also help ensure your park is safe. Penn State Extension offers a simple
   soil test procedure: [agsci.psu.edu/aasl/soil-testing](https://agsci.psu.edu/aasl/soil-testing/fertility/soil-fertility-submission-forms).
2. **Photograph.** We all see the landscape differently, and taking photographs is one way of sharing with others
   what we see. Set aside 30 minutes to walk around and take photos of the park site and surrounding context. Then
   share your photos with one another and describe why you took each one.
3. **Sit and listen.** Sit quietly in the park site and listen with all your senses. What can you hear, smell, and
   touch? After 30 minutes, record what you sensed. Are there positive senses that can be incorporated into the
   park design, or negative senses that could be lessened by it? How would it be different on a different day of
   the week, or time of day or night?
4. **Measure + evaluate.** Taking measurements of the park site is a way to help understand it in more detail — the
   next few steps walk through that.

{/* site-added: safety note (DESIGN.md §2, 2026-10-04) — Penn State's standard fertility test has no lead in it; facts checked 2026-10-04 on agsci.psu.edu and phila.gov. */}
<Callout kind="site">
  Penn State's standard soil fertility test (pH and nutrients) doesn't test for lead. Lead is a separate test on the
  lab's [environmental soil testing form ↗](https://agsci.psu.edu/aasl/soil-testing/environmental), or any
  EPA-recognized lab can do it. The City's [lead guide ↗](https://www.phila.gov/guides/lead-guide/where-is-lead/)
  covers lead in garden soil: covering bare soil, raised beds for food, and testing.
</Callout>

## Diagram your lot

You will now create a diagram of the current park boundaries. This diagram will be used to record dimensions for
objects (like trees, etc.) that are on your site. The diagram is a "plan view," viewed from directly above, similar
to the Google Maps aerial.

The boundary diagram uses grid paper (also called graph paper) to represent the ground, with each small grid
segment representing 1 foot of actual length: draw the long side first (the longest edge of the park, from the
starting point), then the short side at the end of it, then repeat for the other long and short edges.

## Verify your diagram

The next step is to visit the park site to measure and record the length of each property boundary by hand.
**Field-located dimensions measured on-site should be trusted over digital dimensions.**

1. Locate the project starting point — the same location as where you started in Google Maps (or where the site
   marked it for you).
2. Using a 100-foot tape measure, measure and record the distance along the long outside edge of the park, corner
   to corner.
3. Using a 100-foot tape measure, measure and record the distance along the short outside edge, corner to corner.
4. Repeat for the other long edge.
5. Repeat for the other short edge.

## Record & assess objects

The next step is to record the locations of all objects on the project site, and then assess their condition and
potential value to the future park. Objects are located by taking two measurements each: distance from the project
starting point along the edge, and distance from the park edge. This is also a good opportunity to note objects
immediately adjacent to, but outside, the project site — a utility pole, hydrant, or edge of sidewalk that might
influence the park plan.

There are many ways to assess an object once it's located: beauty/character, expected longevity, stability,
potential liability, re-use potential, habitat value, etc. For each one, decide whether it should be kept.

## Sun & shade / base plan

Draw the objects you'd like to keep onto your base plan, the same way you measured them: like a treasure map, place
a dot or an "X" to mark the spot, with a circle around it sized to its footprint (for a tree or shrub, the circle
is the approximate edge of the leaf canopy, with a dot at the trunk).

Then describe the site's conditions on the same diagram:

- For building shadows, draw the approximate outer edge of the shady area and hatch it in.
- For buildings, draw the outline and hatch it in.
- For overhead utilities, label the location above the site.
- For areas with distinct ground conditions (like wet areas), draw a blob shape at the approximate location.
- For trees, label with the type of tree if you know it (e.g. "Maple tree").
